The page

What the page needs.

Five things, in the order a customer looks for them. If your current page has them, it is doing its job. If it is missing some, start there.

The way to book, first.

Walk-in or book, say which, and put the link or the number in the first screen. If you already take bookings on Booksy, Square or Vagaro, the button goes straight there. Someone searching for a barber on a Tuesday night is deciding in seconds. If the booking is a tap away they book; if it is behind a menu they go to the next shop.

Prices with the time each cut takes.

A price list with the minutes beside it tells people two things: what it costs, and that you know what you are doing. Hiding prices behind "DM for pricing" reads as a trick, and it costs you the people who would have paid.

Hours, including the days you are closed.

The hours on the page, the hours on Google and the hours on the door must agree. When they do not, the review that follows is about the locked door, not the cut.

The real chair and the real barber.

One photo of your shop beats twenty stock photos of models. People book a person. Put your name on the page and the chair in the picture.

Where to park and what you take.

The cross street, where the parking is, cash or card, the late and no-show policy in one sentence each. The questions people text you are the questions the page should answer first.

The usual

The mistakes that cost bookings.

  • Instagram as the website. No hours, no prices, no booking link that works without an account, and Google cannot read it.
  • A booking app page with nothing around it. It takes the booking but says nothing about the shop, so people compare on price alone.
  • A template with stock photos of men who have never been in your shop.
  • "We provide quality cuts in a friendly atmosphere." Words that could be any shop's, so they say nothing about yours.
  • The hours on the page from two years ago.
